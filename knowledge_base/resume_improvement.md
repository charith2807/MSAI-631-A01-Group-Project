## Structure and length
Use a reverse-chronological layout with standard headings: Summary, Experience, Education, Skills, and optionally Projects or Certifications. Early-career applicants should fit on one page; two pages is acceptable with several years of relevant experience. Put the most relevant material in the top third of the page, because recruiters skim a résumé in seconds.

## Writing strong bullets
Start every bullet with a strong action verb such as built, led, analyzed, reduced, or designed. Follow the pattern action, then what you did, then the result. Add a number or concrete outcome where you honestly can, for example the percentage of time saved, the number of users, or the size of the dataset. Avoid vague phrases like "responsible for" or "worked on".

## Tailoring a résumé to a job
Read the job description and list the required skills and responsibilities. Reorder your bullets so the most relevant experience comes first, and mirror the posting's terminology for skills you really have. Never add a skill, tool, or result you do not have. If a required skill is missing, describe adjacent experience truthfully and plan to learn the skill instead of claiming it.

## Projects and limited experience
If you have little work experience, lead with Projects, coursework, research, or volunteer work. Describe each project with the problem, your role, the tools used, and the outcome. Link to a public repository or portfolio when it helps a reviewer verify your work.

## Common mistakes
Typos, inconsistent date formats, an objective statement that talks only about what you want, long paragraphs instead of bullets, and listing every tool you have ever touched. Proofread twice and ask another person to read it.
